Thermochemistry from frequencies

Goal: turn an electronic energy and a list of frequencies into zero-point energy, enthalpy, entropy and Gibbs energy at any temperature, and know which parts of that calculation are fragile.

Step 1 · See it

Where a molecule's entropy comes from

The electronic energy \(E\) is the bottom of a well. Real molecules at temperature \(T\) also translate, rotate and vibrate, and each kind of motion adds energy and entropy. In the ideal-gas, rigid-rotor, harmonic-oscillator (RRHO) model, each contribution follows from a handful of numbers: the mass, the rotational constants, the symmetry number and the frequencies. Here is water, computed exactly that way.

Inputs: mass 18.011 amu, rotational constants 27.88, 14.51 and 9.28 cm⁻¹, symmetry number 2, vibrational wavenumbers 1595, 3657 and 3756 cm⁻¹. At 298.15 K the total entropy is 188.7 J/(mol·K); the experimental standard entropy is 188.8.

Translation dominates the entropy, rotation adds a solid share, and water's three stiff vibrations contribute almost nothing until the temperature is high. Soft vibrations behave very differently, as the second figure in Step 3 shows: they can contribute more entropy than a whole rotational degree of freedom, and they are the least reliable numbers in the calculation.

Step 2 · Calculate it

Zero-point energy and vibrational entropy by hand

  1. Zero-point energy. A quantum oscillator never sits at the bottom of its well; its lowest level is \(\tfrac12 h c\tilde\nu\) above it. Summing over modes: \[ \text{ZPE} = \tfrac12\sum_k hc\tilde\nu_k, \qquad 1\ \text{cm}^{-1} = 2.859\times10^{-3}\ \text{kcal/mol} \] For water, \(\tfrac12(1595 + 3657 + 3756) = 4504\) cm⁻¹ \(= 12.88\) kcal/mol. That is a large number to add to a barrier or reaction energy, and it changes between reactant and transition state.
  2. The key variable for each mode is \(x = hc\tilde\nu/k_BT\): the vibrational energy spacing compared with thermal energy. With \(hc/k_B = 1.4388\) cm·K, \(x = 1.4388\,\tilde\nu/T\).
  3. Vibrational entropy of one harmonic mode: \[ \frac{S_\text{vib}}{R} = \frac{x}{e^{x} - 1} - \ln\left(1 - e^{-x}\right) \]
  4. At 298.15 K, compare a stiff and a soft mode:
    ν̃ / cm⁻¹xS / RS / J·mol⁻¹K⁻¹−TS / kcal·mol⁻¹
    10004.8260.0470.39−0.03
    500.2412.42420.16−1.44
    One 50 cm⁻¹ torsion lowers the Gibbs energy by about 1.4 kcal/mol through entropy alone.
  5. Putting it together. Programs report \[ H = E + \text{ZPE} + E_\text{trans} + E_\text{rot} + E_\text{vib}(T) + RT, \qquad G = H - TS \] with \(E_\text{trans} = E_\text{rot} = \tfrac32 RT\) for a non-linear molecule, and \(S = S_\text{trans} + S_\text{rot} + S_\text{vib} + S_\text{elec}\).
Key idea. Stiff modes contribute zero-point energy; soft modes contribute entropy. The same list of frequencies controls both, which is why accurate low frequencies matter for free energies.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

The fragile parts of a free energy

The harmonic formula gives entropy that grows without limit as \(\tilde\nu \to 0\). Real soft modes (methyl rotations, chain torsions, intermolecular motions) are closer to free rotors, and their computed frequencies are the noisiest in the output. The quasi-RRHO approach (Grimme) blends the harmonic entropy into a free-rotor entropy below about 100 cm⁻¹.

harmonic oscillatorquasi-RRHO (ω₀ = 100 cm⁻¹)free rotor
  • Use the reaction temperature. Thermal decomposition happens at several hundred kelvin. Every \(-TS\) term grows with \(T\), so free energies at 298 K can give the wrong picture for high-temperature chemistry. Recompute the thermochemistry at the relevant \(T\); it costs nothing once the frequencies exist.
  • Fragmentation is entropy-driven. When one molecule becomes two, a whole extra set of translations and rotations appears. At 600–700 K the \(-T\Delta S\) term of such a step is large and favourable, and it can outweigh a considerable enthalpy cost.
  • Standard states. Gas-phase outputs use 1 bar (or 1 atm). For reactions in solution the 1 mol/L standard state is used; the conversion adds about 1.9 kcal/mol (\(RT\ln 24.8\) at 298 K) to the free energy of every species. It cancels for unimolecular steps but not when the number of molecules changes.
  • Symmetry numbers and electronic degeneracy. The rotational entropy contains \(-R\ln\sigma\) (σ = 2 for water, 12 for benzene or methane). An open-shell doublet radical has \(S_\text{elec} = R\ln 2 = 5.76\) J/(mol·K). Both are easy to forget in a homolysis energy.
  • Conformers. A floppy molecule populates many minima. Using only one conformer for the reactant (or the TS) can misplace free energies by more than 1 kcal/mol; Boltzmann averaging over a conformer ensemble fixes this.
  • Scaling factors. Harmonic frequencies are often scaled (lesson 1.7); separate scaling factors exist for ZPE and for frequencies, specific to each functional and basis.
Going deeper: partition functions, hindered rotors and the condensed phase

From partition functions. Everything here follows from the molecular partition function \(q = q_\text{trans}\,q_\text{rot}\,q_\text{vib}\,q_\text{elec}\) through \(U = k_BT^2\,\partial\ln Q/\partial T\) and \(S = k_B\ln Q + U/T\). For the ideal gas, \(q_\text{trans} = (2\pi mk_BT/h^2)^{3/2}\,k_BT/p\), and for a non-linear rigid rotor \(q_\text{rot} = \tfrac{\sqrt\pi}{\sigma}(k_BT/hc)^{3/2}/\sqrt{ABC}\). The vibrational formula in Step 2 is \(-\ln(1-e^{-x})\) plus its temperature derivative. The figure uses exactly these expressions.

Hindered rotors. A torsion with a barrier comparable to \(k_BT\) is neither harmonic nor free. One-dimensional hindered-rotor treatments compute its energy levels from a scanned torsional potential; quasi-RRHO is a cheaper interpolation that avoids the divergence.

Anharmonicity. VPT2 corrects frequencies and ZPE for anharmonic coupling. For thermochemistry of small molecules this matters at the 0.1–0.5 kcal/mol level; for floppy molecules the low-frequency treatment matters more.

Condensed phases. Gas-phase translational and rotational entropies overestimate what a molecule keeps in a liquid or a polymer melt, where these motions are hindered. Implicit-solvation free energies and various entropy corrections address this, but there is no exact recipe. For reactions inside a polymer, compare computed free energies with this limitation in mind.
