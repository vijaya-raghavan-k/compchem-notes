Intrinsic reaction coordinate

Goal: understand the IRC as the steepest-descent path from a transition state in mass-weighted coordinates, see how masses and step sizes shape it, and use it correctly to confirm what a transition state connects.

Step 1 · See it

Sliding down from a saddle

Start at a transition state, nudge the structure a tiny bit along the imaginary mode in both directions, and follow the steepest descent down each side. The path you trace is the intrinsic reaction coordinate. On the Müller–Brown surface below, give the two coordinates different "masses" and watch the path bend: in mass-weighted coordinates, light atoms move more readily than heavy ones.

With equal masses the IRC is the ordinary steepest-descent path. Make \(y\) heavy and the IRC leaves the saddle along a direction dominated by \(x\), the light coordinate, before curving into the valley. Both versions connect the same two minima here, but the route between them, and the length \(s\), depend on the masses. The pink path shows what happens with crude, large steps: it overshoots across the valley walls instead of following the floor.

Step 2 · Calculate it

Steepest descent in mass-weighted coordinates

  1. Use the mass-weighted coordinates of lesson 1.10, \(q_i = \sqrt{m_i}\,x_i\). Their gradient is \(\partial E/\partial q_i = g_i/\sqrt{m_i}\). The IRC is the steepest-descent path in these coordinates: \[ \frac{d\mathbf q}{ds} = -\frac{\mathbf M^{-1/2}\mathbf g}{|\mathbf M^{-1/2}\mathbf g|} \] where \(s\) is the arc length in mass-weighted units (amu\(^{1/2}\)·Bohr, or amu\(^{1/2}\)·Å).
  2. Back in Cartesian coordinates, \(d\mathbf x = \mathbf M^{-1/2}d\mathbf q\), so the direction of motion is \[ d\mathbf x \propto -\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf g, \qquad dx_i \propto -\frac{g_i}{m_i} \] Each coordinate moves in proportion to its force divided by its mass: exactly like a particle starting from rest, \(a = F/m\).
  3. Example: masses \((1,\ 16)\) and gradient \(\mathbf g = (0.2,\ 0.2)\). Plain steepest descent goes along \((-0.2,\ -0.2)\), at 45°. The IRC goes along \((-0.2/1,\ -0.2/16) = (-0.2,\ -0.0125)\): almost entirely along the light coordinate.
  4. Starting the path. At the transition state \(\mathbf g = \mathbf 0\), so the formula gives no direction. The path leaves along the eigenvector of the mass-weighted Hessian with the negative eigenvalue: the imaginary mode of lesson 1.11, converted to Cartesians as \(\mathbf M^{-1/2}\mathbf v\).
Key idea. The IRC is the path a molecule would follow from the saddle if it moved infinitely slowly, always shedding its kinetic energy. Masses enter because they decide how forces turn into motion.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

Running and reading an IRC

  • Why run it. A single imaginary frequency proves you found a first-order saddle, not that it is the saddle of your reaction. The IRC shows which minima it actually connects. Unexpected endpoints reveal hidden intermediates (like minimum C between A and B here) or a different reaction altogether.
  • Start from a good TS and Hessian. The IRC needs an accurately converged saddle and its Hessian to choose the initial direction. Most programs read the Hessian from the preceding frequency calculation.
  • Step size and integrator. Programs use predictor–corrector integrators (such as Gonzalez–Schlegel) that stay on the path with fairly large steps; the crude pink path above shows why a naive method cannot. Typical steps are around 0.1 amu\(^{1/2}\)·Bohr; smaller steps give smoother profiles near the saddle.
  • The endpoints are not minima. IRCs usually stop when the energy flattens out, before full convergence. Optimize both endpoints, then compare them with your separately optimized reactant and product.
  • The energy profile. Plotting \(E\) against \(s\), as in the lower figure, gives the reaction profile. Its shape shows whether the barrier is early or late, and its curvature near \(s = 0\) is related to the magnitude of the imaginary frequency.
Going deeper: IRC versus MEP, bifurcations and dynamics

Coordinate (in)dependence. Fukui defined the IRC in mass-weighted Cartesian coordinates, where it is unique. Steepest descent in internal coordinates, or in unweighted Cartesians (the "minimum energy path" that NEB converges to), gives different curves away from the stationary points. All of them pass through the same minima and saddle, so energies of stationary points are unaffected; path lengths and shapes are not.

Reaction path curvature. Where the IRC bends sharply, energy can flow from the reaction coordinate into perpendicular vibrations. The reaction path Hamiltonian of Miller, Handy and Adams uses the IRC, its curvature and the frequencies of perpendicular modes along it; it underlies variational transition state theory and small-curvature tunneling methods.

Bifurcations. After a valley-ridge inflection point, the valley floor turns into a ridge and the path can split toward two products. An IRC is a single deterministic curve and will follow only one branch, so it can hide a second product. Product ratios in such cases need molecular dynamics.

Real molecules are not infinitely slow. Molecules cross barriers with kinetic energy, and their trajectories can deviate considerably from the IRC, especially in flat regions after the saddle. The IRC defines the reaction mechanism geometrically; it does not describe the dynamics in time.
