Taylor expansion and the quadratic model

Goal: approximate a surface near one point using its value, gradient and Hessian; understand the harmonic approximation; and derive the Newton step that optimizers are built on, including how it fails.

Step 1 · See it

Polynomials that hug the curve

At one point you know the energy, the slope and the curvature. A Taylor expansion uses exactly these numbers to build a polynomial that matches the curve at that point. A line matches value and slope; a parabola also matches curvature; higher orders match more. Near the point the match is excellent. Far away, it can be very wrong.

Taylor approximations of a bond potentialDrag the expansion point

Put the expansion point at the minimum (0.958 Å). The linear term disappears because the slope is zero, and the 2nd-order curve is the familiar harmonic parabola. It matches well near the bottom but rises far too steeply on the stretching side, where the real bond levels off toward dissociation. The 3rd and 4th orders repair much of this. That difference is anharmonicity.

Step 2 · Calculate it

From the expansion to the Newton step

In one variable, the Taylor series around \(x_0\) with \(\Delta = x - x_0\) is

\[ E(x_0 + \Delta) = E(x_0) + E'(x_0)\,\Delta + \tfrac{1}{2}E''(x_0)\,\Delta^2 + \tfrac{1}{6}E'''(x_0)\,\Delta^3 + \dots \]
  1. Keep terms up to \(\Delta^2\): this is the quadratic model. Find where its slope is zero: \[ \frac{d}{d\Delta}\left(E_0 + E'\Delta + \tfrac12 E''\Delta^2\right) = E' + E''\Delta = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta = -\frac{E'}{E''} \] This is the Newton step: jump to the stationary point of the local parabola.
  2. Apply it to \(E(x) = x^4 - 2x^2\) (true minimum at \(x = 1\)), starting at \(x = 1.5\). There \(E' = 4x^3 - 4x = 7.5\) and \(E'' = 12x^2 - 4 = 23\): \[ x_1 = 1.5 - \frac{7.5}{23} = 1.1739 \]
  3. Repeat from each new point:

  4. Look at the error column: once close, it roughly squares each step (\(10^{-2} \to 10^{-3} \to 10^{-6}\)), so the number of correct digits doubles. This is quadratic convergence, and it is why Newton-type methods finish in a handful of steps when started near a minimum.

The same model in many dimensions

With a displacement vector \(\Delta\mathbf x\), gradient \(\mathbf g\) and Hessian matrix \(\mathbf H\) (lesson 1.8), the quadratic model and its stationary point are

\[ E(\mathbf x_0 + \Delta\mathbf x) \approx E_0 + \mathbf g^{\mathsf T}\Delta\mathbf x + \tfrac12\,\Delta\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H\,\Delta\mathbf x, \qquad \Delta\mathbf x_\text{Newton} = -\mathbf H^{-1}\mathbf g \]

At a minimum \(\mathbf g = \mathbf 0\), and what remains, \(E \approx E_0 + \tfrac12\Delta\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H\Delta\mathbf x\), is the harmonic approximation. Every standard frequency calculation is built on this one line.

Key idea. Gradient plus Hessian give a local quadratic model. Optimizers jump to its stationary point; frequency calculations read its curvature.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

Optimizers, trust radii and the harmonic approximation

Run Newton's method yourself on the double well from lesson 1.6. Each step draws the local parabola and jumps to its stationary point.

Newton's method on a double wellTap to choose a start, then step

Start near \(x = 0.3\) and press step: Newton runs uphill to the barrier top. The model there is an upside-down parabola, and its stationary point is a maximum. Pure Newton converges to the nearest stationary point of any kind. Real optimizers therefore modify the step:

  • Trust radius. Never step farther than a distance where the model is trusted. After each step, compare the predicted energy change with the actual one; if they disagree, shrink the radius, otherwise grow it.
  • Curvature control. For a minimum search the model's curvature must be positive in every direction. Methods such as rational function optimization (RFO) shift the step so it always goes downhill. Transition-state searches do the opposite on purpose: uphill along one direction, downhill along all others (lesson 2.4).
  • Approximate Hessians. Computing \(\mathbf H\) exactly at every step is expensive, so quasi-Newton methods (BFGS) build it up from the gradients they already have (lesson 2.1).

Why frequencies need an optimized geometry

The harmonic analysis assumes the linear term \(\mathbf g^{\mathsf T}\Delta\mathbf x\) is zero. At a geometry that is not a stationary point of the same method and basis set, it is not, and the resulting frequencies are not meaningful (the low ones are affected most). This is why a geometry optimized at one level should be re-optimized before computing frequencies at another.

Harmonic versus real frequencies

Computed harmonic frequencies are usually somewhat higher than measured fundamentals, partly because of anharmonicity (the 3rd and 4th-order terms above) and partly because of method error. Empirical scaling factors (often 0.94–0.99 depending on functional and basis) correct for both on average. Explicit anharmonic methods such as VPT2 use third and fourth derivatives instead.

Going deeper: convergence rate, error terms and the trust ratio

Why convergence is quadratic. Write \(e_k = x_k - x^\ast\). Expanding \(E'\) around the minimum in the Newton formula gives \(e_{k+1} \approx \dfrac{E'''(x^\ast)}{2E''(x^\ast)}\,e_k^2\). For \(x^4 - 2x^2\): \(E''' = 24\), \(E'' = 8\), so \(e_{k+1} \approx 1.5\,e_k^2\). Check against the table: \(1.5 \times (0.0323)^2 = 0.00157\), close to the observed 0.00146.

The remainder term. Truncating after \(\Delta^2\) leaves an error of order \(\tfrac16 E'''\Delta^3\). For a Morse bond \(E''' = -6D_ea^3\) at the minimum, so the harmonic model underestimates the energy on compression and overestimates it on stretching by about \(D_ea^3\Delta^3\).

The trust ratio. Optimizers compute \(\rho = \Delta E_\text{actual}/\Delta E_\text{predicted}\). Values near 1 mean the quadratic model is reliable and the trust radius can grow; small or negative values mean the step was too ambitious. This single number is what controls the step-size logic in most modern geometry optimizers.

Cost. Inverting or factorizing an \(n\times n\) Hessian costs order \(n^3\) operations, trivial for \(n = 3N\) of a few hundred. The expensive part is computing \(\mathbf H\) in the first place: an analytic DFT Hessian costs many times a gradient, and a numerical one costs \(6N\) gradients (lesson 1.4).
