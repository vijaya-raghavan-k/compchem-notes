Transition state theory and kinetics

Goal: convert a computed free-energy barrier into a rate constant with the Eyring equation, relate it to Arrhenius parameters, and understand how errors, temperature and tunneling affect the comparison with experiment.

Step 1 · See it

From a barrier to a rate

Transition state theory says the rate of a unimolecular step is an attempt frequency, \(k_BT/h\), times the fraction of molecules that have reached the transition state, \(e^{-\Delta G^\ddagger/RT}\). Splitting \(\Delta G^\ddagger = \Delta H^\ddagger - T\Delta S^\ddagger\) shows where the two Arrhenius parameters come from. Change them and watch the rate and the Arrhenius plot respond.

Three things to notice. Each extra kcal/mol of barrier costs a constant factor in rate at fixed temperature, and that factor is much smaller at 600 K than at 300 K. A negative \(\Delta S^\ddagger\), typical of ordered cyclic transition states, lowers the pre-exponential factor without changing the slope. And tunneling bends the Arrhenius plot upward at low temperature, where the Wigner factor grows; at high temperature the line is straight again.

Step 2 · Calculate it

Rate constants by hand

  1. The Eyring equation for a unimolecular step: \[ k = \kappa\,\frac{k_BT}{h}\,e^{-\Delta G^\ddagger/RT} \] At 298.15 K, \(k_BT/h = 6.21\times10^{12}\) s⁻¹ and \(RT = 0.5925\) kcal/mol. The transmission coefficient \(\kappa\) is 1 unless tunneling or recrossing is included.
  2. For \(\Delta G^\ddagger = 20\) kcal/mol at 298 K: \(20/0.5925 = 33.76\), \(e^{-33.76} = 2.19\times10^{-15}\), so \[ k = 6.21\times10^{12}\times 2.19\times10^{-15} = 0.0136\ \text{s}^{-1}, \qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k} = 51\ \text{s} \]
  3. Rule of thumb. One factor of 10 in rate corresponds to \(RT\ln 10\): 1.36 kcal/mol at 298 K, but 2.75 kcal/mol at 600 K. A 1 kcal/mol error in the barrier changes \(k\) by a factor of 5.4 at 298 K and only 2.3 at 600 K.
  4. Arrhenius parameters. Comparing \(k = A\,e^{-E_a/RT}\) with Eyring, using \(E_a = RT^2\,d\ln k/dT\), gives for a unimolecular gas-phase step \[ E_a = \Delta H^\ddagger + RT, \qquad A = \frac{e\,k_BT}{h}\,e^{\Delta S^\ddagger/R} \] At 600 K with \(\Delta H^\ddagger = 40\) kcal/mol and \(\Delta S^\ddagger = -5\) cal/(mol·K): \(E_a = 41.2\) kcal/mol and \(A = 3.40\times10^{13}\times e^{-2.52} = 2.7\times10^{12}\) s⁻¹.
Key idea. \(\Delta H^\ddagger\) sets the slope of the Arrhenius plot; \(\Delta S^\ddagger\) sets its height. A computed electronic barrier \(\Delta E^\ddagger\) is neither \(E_a\) nor \(\Delta G^\ddagger\) until the thermal corrections of lesson 2.6 are added.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

Computing and comparing rates

  1. Free energies at the right temperature. \(\Delta G^\ddagger = G(\text{TS}) - G(\text{reactant})\), both from frequency calculations at the same level, temperature and standard state (lesson 2.6).
  2. The right reactant. Use the lowest-energy reactant conformer (or a Boltzmann-weighted ensemble), not the conformer that happens to look like the TS. If several conformers interconvert quickly, the barrier is measured from the most stable one (Curtin–Hammett).
  3. Bimolecular steps have units of concentration⁻¹ time⁻¹, and the standard state (1 bar or 1 mol/L) enters the result through \(\Delta G^\ddagger\).
  4. Tunneling. The imaginary frequency from lesson 1.11 gives the simplest correction, Wigner's \(\kappa = 1 + \tfrac1{24}(hc|\tilde\nu^\ddagger|/k_BT)^2\). It is reasonable when \(\kappa\) is small (high temperature, modest frequency). Large values call for Eckart or small-curvature methods.
Bridge to my own work: computed barriers and experimental activation energies. Thermal analysis of polymers (TGA with Kissinger, Friedman or Ozawa–Flynn–Wall analysis) gives an apparent activation energy for mass loss. It describes the whole condensed-phase process, which may combine several elementary steps, diffusion of volatiles and changing chain ends. A computed value refers to one elementary step of a model molecule in the gas phase. Compare like with like: convert the computed barrier to \(E_a = \Delta H^\ddagger + RT\) at the experimental temperature, consider which elementary step limits the observed rate, and remember that at ~600 K a 1 kcal/mol error is only a factor of about 2 in rate. Agreement within a few kcal/mol is meaningful; exact agreement can be coincidental.
Going deeper: assumptions of transition state theory and beyond

Derivation in one line. Assume the transition-state species is in quasi-equilibrium with reactants and that every crossing of the dividing surface is reactive. Factoring the motion along the reaction coordinate out of the TS partition function gives \(k = \frac{k_BT}{h}\,\frac{Q^\ddagger}{Q_R}\,e^{-\Delta E_0/k_BT}\), where \(Q^\ddagger\) excludes the imaginary mode and \(\Delta E_0\) is the ZPE-corrected barrier. The Gibbs-energy form is the same equation in thermodynamic language.

Recrossing and variational TST. Trajectories can cross the dividing surface and come back, so conventional TST is an upper bound. Variational TST places the dividing surface at the free-energy maximum along the IRC (lesson 2.5) at each temperature, which minimizes recrossing. It matters most for loose transition states, such as barrierless radical recombinations.

Pressure dependence. TST assumes energy is redistributed fast. At low pressure, unimolecular reactions become limited by collisional activation (falloff). RRKM theory with a master equation treats this regime.

Networks of steps. For a mechanism with several steps, the observed rate depends on which barrier is highest relative to the lowest preceding intermediate. The energetic span model formalizes this for catalytic cycles; for decomposition networks, microkinetic modeling solves all rate equations together.

Kissinger in TST language. The Kissinger relation \(\ln(\beta/T_p^2) = \text{const} - E_a/RT_p\), with heating rate \(\beta\) and peak temperature \(T_p\), assumes a single Arrhenius process. Its slope gives an \(E_a\) comparable to \(\Delta H^\ddagger + RT\) only if one elementary step really dominates the measured mass loss.
