Second derivatives and curvature

Goal: understand why a zero gradient is not enough to identify a minimum, read the second derivative as curvature (a force constant), and see that in two or more dimensions the curvatures along the coordinate axes can be misleading.

Step 1 · See it

Bending up or bending down

At the bottom of a well and at the top of a barrier the slope is zero in both cases. What tells them apart is how the curve bends. The second derivative \(E''\) is the rate of change of the slope: positive when the curve bends upward like a cup, negative when it bends downward like a cap. Near any point, the curve looks like a parabola whose opening is set by \(E''\).

Jump to the three stationary points. At both wells \(E' = 0\) and \(E'' > 0\): minima. At the barrier top \(E' = 0\) and \(E'' < 0\): a maximum. Between them are two inflection points where \(E'' = 0\) and the bending changes sign; the shaded region between them bends downward.

Step 2 · Calculate it

Classifying stationary points by hand

  1. Take \(E(x) = x^3 - 3x\). First derivative: \(E'(x) = 3x^2 - 3\), which is zero at \(x = 1\) and \(x = -1\). These are the stationary points.
  2. Second derivative: \(E''(x) = 6x\). At \(x = 1\), \(E'' = 6 > 0\): a minimum. At \(x = -1\), \(E'' = -6 < 0\): a maximum.
  3. Numerically, with values only, the central second difference is \[ E''(x) \approx \frac{E(x+h) - 2E(x) + E(x-h)}{h^2} \] At \(x = 1\), \(h = 0.1\): \(\dfrac{-1.969 - 2(-2) + (-1.971)}{0.01} = \dfrac{0.060}{0.01} = 6.0\).
  4. Curvature is a force constant. Near a minimum, \(E \approx E_0 + \tfrac12 k (x - x_0)^2\) with \(k = E''(x_0)\). For a Morse bond (lesson 1.3), \(k = 2D_e a^2 = 2 \times 120 \times 2.2^2 = 1162\) kcal/mol/Å², which is 8.07 mdyn/Å, typical of an O–H bond.
  5. A force constant and a mass give a frequency. With \(k\) in mdyn/Å and the reduced mass \(\mu\) in amu, \[ \tilde\nu = 1302.8\ \sqrt{k/\mu}\ \ \text{cm}^{-1}, \qquad \mu_\mathrm{OH} = \frac{16.00 \times 1.008}{17.01} = 0.948 \ \Rightarrow\ \tilde\nu = 1302.8\sqrt{8.07/0.948} \approx 3800\ \text{cm}^{-1} \] The constant 1302.8 is \(\sqrt{(10^{2}\ \text{N/m})/(1\ \text{amu})}\,/\,(2\pi c)\) written in cm⁻¹.

Two dimensions: the trap

With two coordinates there are three second derivatives: \(E_{xx}\), \(E_{yy}\) and the mixed one \(E_{xy} = \partial^2 E/\partial x\,\partial y\) (which equals \(E_{yx}\) for smooth functions). Take \(E = x^2 + 4xy + y^2\):

\[ E_{xx} = 2,\quad E_{yy} = 2,\quad E_{xy} = 4 \]

Both axis curvatures are positive, so it looks like a bowl. But along the diagonal \(y = -x\), \(E = x^2 - 4x^2 + x^2 = -2x^2\): downhill. The point is a saddle. The test that catches this uses the discriminant

\[ D = E_{xx}E_{yy} - E_{xy}^2 = 4 - 16 = -12 < 0 \ \Rightarrow\ \text{saddle} \]

\(D > 0\) with \(E_{xx} > 0\) is a minimum; \(D > 0\) with \(E_{xx} < 0\) is a maximum; \(D < 0\) is a saddle.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

Minima, transition states and hidden directions

Play with the three second derivatives of a two-coordinate surface \(E = \tfrac12(E_{xx}x^2 + 2E_{xy}xy + E_{yy}y^2)\) and watch the shape change. Blue regions are above the stationary point, pink regions below it.

Three second derivatives, one shapeMove the sliders or pick a preset
  • A minimum (a stable structure) has positive curvature in every direction.
  • A transition state is a first-order saddle: positive curvature in every direction except exactly one, which runs along the reaction path.
  • In a molecule there are \(3N - 6\) directions and the mixed derivatives couple all of them, so the discriminant trick no longer applies. The general tool, which finds the "true" directions of curvature hidden by the coupling, is the eigenvalue analysis of lessons 1.8 and 1.9.
  • Curvature also explains the shape of bond potentials. A Morse curve has \(E'' < 0\) beyond its inflection point at \(r = r_e + \ln 2/a\) (about 0.32 Å past equilibrium for the O–H model). A strongly stretched bond lies in a region of negative curvature, which is what eventually lets it break.
Going deeper: where the test comes from, and why saddles must exist

The discriminant is a determinant. Collect the second derivatives in a symmetric table \(\begin{pmatrix} E_{xx} & E_{xy} \\ E_{xy} & E_{yy}\end{pmatrix}\): this is the 2×2 Hessian matrix, and \(D\) is its determinant. The determinant equals the product of the matrix's two eigenvalues (lesson 1.9), so \(D < 0\) means one eigenvalue is positive and one negative: one uphill and one downhill direction.

Symmetry of mixed derivatives. If \(E\) has continuous second derivatives, \(E_{xy} = E_{yx}\) (Schwarz's theorem). This is why every Hessian is symmetric. A numerically computed Hessian is slightly asymmetric because of noise, and programs symmetrize it by averaging \(H_{ij}\) and \(H_{ji}\).

Curvature versus second derivative. The geometric curvature of a curve is \(\kappa = E''/(1 + E'^2)^{3/2}\). At stationary points \(E' = 0\) and the two agree, which is the only place chemists usually care about. Away from stationary points the second derivative still exists, but frequencies computed there are not meaningful (lesson 1.10).

Why transition states exist. On a continuous surface with two separate minima, every path between them has a highest point, and the path whose highest point is lowest passes through a saddle point (the "mountain pass" theorem). Saddles are therefore guaranteed, not accidents. The one with the lowest highest point defines the barrier.
