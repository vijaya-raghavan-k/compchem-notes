Convergence criteria

Goal: know what the energy, gradient and step thresholds each test, see why a geometry can pass a test while still being degrees away from the minimum along a soft coordinate, and choose criteria for the job.

Step 1 · See it

Where an optimizer would stop

Take a molecule reduced to two coordinates: a stiff bond (force constant 1 Hartree/Å²) and a soft torsion. The point you drag is a geometry near the minimum, displaced by \(\delta x\) along the bond and \(\delta\varphi\) along the torsion. The program checks five numbers there. The green region shows every geometry where all five pass: the places where the optimizer would declare "converged".

Step 2 · Calculate it

What each criterion measures

  1. The five tests. At each step the program compares the gradient \(\mathbf g\), the proposed step \(\mathbf s\) and the energy change against thresholds: \[ \max_i|g_i| \le G,\quad \text{rms}(\mathbf g) \le \tfrac23 G,\quad \max_i|s_i| \le S,\quad \text{rms}(\mathbf s) \le \tfrac23 S,\quad |\Delta E| \le E_\text{thr} \] The 2/3 ratio between RMS and maximum thresholds is a common convention; exact definitions differ between programs.
  2. Example. A two-atom gradient \(\mathbf g = (0.4, -1.2, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1, -0.5)\times10^{-3}\) Hartree/Å. Max \(= 1.2\times10^{-3}\), which fails a \(10^{-3}\) threshold. RMS \(= \sqrt{(0.16 + 1.44 + 0.09 + 0.04 + 0.01 + 0.25)\times10^{-6}/6} = 5.8\times10^{-4}\), which passes \(\tfrac23\times10^{-3}\). One component is enough to keep the optimization going.
  3. How far from the minimum is a small gradient? Near a minimum, along one coordinate with curvature \(k\), the gradient is \(g = k\,\delta\). So the remaining geometry error and energy error are \[ \delta = \frac{g}{k}, \qquad \delta E = \tfrac12 k\delta^2 = \frac{g^2}{2k} \]
  4. At the same gradient, \(g = 10^{-3}\):
    Coordinatekerror δenergy error
    stiff bond1.0 Hartree/Å²0.001 Å5 × 10⁻⁷ Hartree
    soft torsion0.004 Hartree/rad²0.25 rad = 14°1.25 × 10⁻⁴ Hartree
    The same gradient threshold leaves the bond essentially exact and the torsion 14° off.
  5. Why the step and energy tests exist. With a good Hessian, the proposed Newton step is \(s = g/k = \delta\): it measures the remaining geometry error directly, so the step test catches the torsion. The predicted energy change \(\tfrac12 g\,s = g^2/2k\) catches it too. Both work only if the optimizer's Hessian is right, which is why a poor Hessian can produce false convergence.
Key idea. A gradient threshold bounds force, not position. On soft coordinates, small forces allow large geometry errors, and only the step and energy tests (with a reasonable Hessian) reveal them.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

Choosing thresholds for the job

Programs offer preset quality levels. The figure uses four levels, each ten times tighter than the last, similar to AMS's named quality settings:

LevelEnergy / HartreeMax gradient / Hartree·Å⁻¹Max step / Å
Basic10⁻⁴10⁻²0.1
Normal10⁻⁵10⁻³0.01
Good10⁻⁶10⁻⁴0.001
Very good10⁻⁷10⁻⁵0.0001

Gaussian's defaults, for comparison: max force 4.5 × 10⁻⁴ and RMS force 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ Hartree/Bohr, max displacement 1.8 × 10⁻³ and RMS displacement 1.2 × 10⁻³ Bohr. Always check units (Bohr or Å) and the exact definitions in your program's manual.

  • Before frequencies, thermochemistry or transition states, use a tighter level. Residual error along soft modes shows up as small imaginary frequencies (lesson 1.11) and distorts the low-frequency entropies that dominate free energies (lesson 2.6).
  • Floppy molecules and weak complexes (long chains, methyl rotors, hydrogen-bonded dimers) have very soft modes. Expect slow convergence and check that low frequencies are stable after re-optimizing.
  • The noise floor. Gradients carry numerical noise from SCF convergence and integration grids. If the noise is near the gradient threshold, the optimizer wanders and oscillates instead of converging. The fix is a finer grid and tighter SCF, not more optimization steps. The SCF must always be converged well beyond the geometry criteria.
  • Energies converge first. The energy error is quadratic in the geometry error, so reaction energies are usually fine at Normal settings, while geometries and frequencies benefit from tighter ones.
Going deeper: error propagation and false convergence

Geometry error from the gradient. In many dimensions \(\boldsymbol\delta \approx \mathbf H^{-1}\mathbf g\). Writing \(\mathbf g\) in the Hessian's eigenvectors, each component is divided by its eigenvalue, so the geometry error is dominated by the lowest-curvature modes. The ratio of largest to smallest curvature (the condition number, lesson 1.5) sets how unevenly converged a structure is.

Energy converges quadratically. \(\delta E \approx \tfrac12\boldsymbol\delta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H\boldsymbol\delta = \tfrac12\mathbf g^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H^{-1}\mathbf g\). Halving the gradient divides the energy error by four. This is why comparing energies across conformers needs much less geometric precision than comparing low-frequency modes.

False convergence. The step and energy tests use the optimizer's approximate Hessian. If it overestimates the curvature of a soft mode (common with generic model Hessians for unusual torsions or weak contacts), predicted steps and energy changes look small and the run stops early. Checks: re-run from the "converged" geometry with a fresh Hessian, or compute frequencies; a lowest frequency that changes a lot after re-optimizing indicates the original was not converged.

Effect on frequencies. At a non-stationary point, the projected Hessian includes gradient-dependent terms, and residual displacement along a soft mode shifts its curvature through third-derivative (anharmonic) coupling. Low frequencies are the most sensitive, with errors of tens of cm⁻¹ common for loosely converged floppy molecules.
