Mass weighting and normal modes

Goal: derive why the Hessian must be mass-weighted before diagonalizing, see how masses and force constants together set the vibrational frequencies and mode shapes, and follow every step a program takes from Hessian to cm⁻¹.

Step 1 · See it

A linear triatomic on springs

Take three atoms on a line, like CO₂ moving only along its axis, joined by two identical springs. The Hessian (lesson 1.8) only knows about the springs. But how the atoms actually move also depends on how heavy they are: a light atom on a stiff spring vibrates fast, a heavy one slowly. Change the masses below and watch the modes and frequencies respond, while the Hessian stays exactly the same.

Normal modes of a linear A–B–A moleculePick a mode, then change the masses

With CO₂ masses you get a symmetric stretch (the carbon stays still), an antisymmetric stretch (the carbon moves against both oxygens) and a zero-frequency mode, which is simply the whole molecule sliding along the line. Now tick "skip mass weighting": the program diagonalizes the bare Hessian, and the modes come out with the centre of mass moving and frequencies that ignore the masses entirely. That is the error mass weighting prevents.

Step 2 · Calculate it

From Newton's law to an eigenvalue problem

  1. Near a minimum, the force is \(\mathbf F = -\mathbf H\Delta\mathbf x\) (lesson 1.8). Newton's law for every coordinate at once, with the masses on a diagonal matrix \(\mathbf M\), reads \[ \mathbf M\,\ddot{\mathbf x} = -\mathbf H\,\mathbf x \] The masses multiply the accelerations, so a plain eigen-decomposition of \(\mathbf H\) cannot be the whole story.
  2. Change variables to mass-weighted coordinates \(\mathbf q = \mathbf M^{1/2}\mathbf x\), that is \(q_i = \sqrt{m_i}\,x_i\). Multiplying both sides by \(\mathbf M^{-1/2}\): \[ \ddot{\mathbf q} = -\underbrace{\mathbf M^{-1/2}\,\mathbf H\,\mathbf M^{-1/2}}_{\mathbf H_\text{mw}}\,\mathbf q, \qquad (\mathbf H_\text{mw})_{ij} = \frac{H_{ij}}{\sqrt{m_i m_j}} \]
  3. Try a solution in which every coordinate oscillates together: \(\mathbf q(t) = \mathbf v\cos\omega t\). Then \(\ddot{\mathbf q} = -\omega^2\mathbf q\), and the equation becomes \[ \mathbf H_\text{mw}\,\mathbf v = \omega^2\,\mathbf v \] An eigenvalue problem (lesson 1.9). Each eigenvector is a normal mode; each eigenvalue is the square of its angular frequency.
  4. Diatomic, by hand. Two atoms, one spring: \(\mathbf H = k\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Mass-weighting gives \[ \mathbf H_\text{mw} = k\begin{pmatrix} 1/m_1 & -1/\sqrt{m_1m_2} \\ -1/\sqrt{m_1m_2} & 1/m_2 \end{pmatrix} \] Its trace is \(k(1/m_1 + 1/m_2) = k/\mu\) and its determinant is zero. So the eigenvalues are \(0\) (translation) and \(k/\mu\): the familiar \(\omega = \sqrt{k/\mu}\) with reduced mass \(\mu = m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)\) appears automatically.
  5. Numbers for H³⁵Cl. \(k = 5.16\) mdyn/Å, \(\mu = 1.008 \times 34.969/35.977 = 0.9798\) amu: \[ \tilde\nu = 1302.8\sqrt{5.16/0.9798} = 1302.8 \times 2.295 = 2990\ \text{cm}^{-1} \]
Key idea. The Hessian holds the springs; the masses decide how the atoms respond. Mass-weighting combines both into one symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues are \(\omega^2\).

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

What a frequency calculation actually does

  1. Hessian. At an optimized geometry, compute the \(3N\times3N\) Cartesian Hessian, analytically or by differencing gradients (lesson 1.8). Units: Hartree/Bohr².
  2. Mass-weight. Divide each element by \(\sqrt{m_im_j}\), using isotope masses in amu. Choosing a different isotope changes only this step.
  3. Project. Remove the 3 translations and 3 rotations (2 for linear molecules) so they appear as exact zeros rather than noisy small numbers.
  4. Diagonalize. Find the eigenvalues \(\lambda_k = \omega_k^2\) and eigenvectors \(\mathbf v_k\) of \(\mathbf H_\text{mw}\).
  5. Convert. With \(\lambda\) in Hartree/(Bohr² amu), \[ \tilde\nu_k = \frac{\omega_k}{2\pi c} = 5140.48\,\sqrt{\lambda_k}\ \ \text{cm}^{-1} \] (the constant is \(\sqrt{E_h/(a_0^2\,\text{amu})}/(2\pi c)\)). A negative \(\lambda\) has no real square root; that case is lesson 1.11.
  6. Report the modes. Programs print each mode as Cartesian displacements \(\mathbf d_k = \mathbf M^{-1/2}\mathbf v_k\), normalized, often with a mode "reduced mass" and "force constant". These are conventions; different programs normalize differently, so compare shapes rather than raw numbers between codes.
  • Orthogonality. Mode vectors \(\mathbf v_k\) are orthogonal in the plain sense; the Cartesian displacements \(\mathbf d_k\) are orthogonal only in the mass-weighted sense, \(\sum_i m_i\,d_{k,i}\,d_{l,i} = 0\) (lesson 1.2).
  • Isotope effects come for free. The potential energy surface, and so the Hessian, does not depend on nuclear mass. Swap H for D and re-run only steps 2–5. Bonds to hydrogen drop by a factor near \(1/\sqrt2\); this shift is the origin of kinetic isotope effects (lesson 2.7).
  • Thermochemistry uses these numbers. The zero-point energy \(\tfrac12\sum_k h c\tilde\nu_k\), vibrational entropy and heat capacity all come from this list of frequencies (lesson 2.6). Low-frequency modes dominate the entropy and are the least accurate, which is why special treatments exist for them.
Going deeper: the generalized eigenproblem, Eckart conditions and the GF method

Equivalent form. Substituting back, \(\mathbf H_\text{mw}\mathbf v = \omega^2\mathbf v\) is the same as the generalized eigenproblem \(\mathbf H\,\mathbf d = \omega^2\,\mathbf M\,\mathbf d\), with \(\mathbf d = \mathbf M^{-1/2}\mathbf v\). The same mathematical structure, \(\mathbf A\mathbf x = \lambda\mathbf B\mathbf x\), reappears in the SCF equations \(\mathbf F\mathbf C = \mathbf S\mathbf C\boldsymbol\varepsilon\), with the overlap matrix playing the role of the mass matrix.

Eckart conditions. Removing rotations exactly requires defining a body-fixed frame. The Eckart conditions choose it so that, to first order, vibrations carry no linear or angular momentum. Projection is exact only at a stationary point; at a non-stationary geometry the gradient couples rotations and vibrations, another reason frequencies need a converged structure.

Wilson's GF method. In internal coordinates, with force constants \(\mathbf F\) and the kinetic matrix \(\mathbf G = \mathbf B\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\) (B from lesson 1.4), the frequencies solve \(\det(\mathbf G\mathbf F - \lambda\mathbf I) = 0\). Spectroscopists used this before Cartesian Hessians were cheap; it is still the clearest way to see which internal coordinates a mode involves (potential energy distribution analysis).

Limits of the picture. Everything here is harmonic. Real fundamentals are lower because of anharmonicity, and modes can mix through Fermi resonance (in real CO₂ the symmetric stretch mixes with the bend overtone, splitting the band near 1340 cm⁻¹). VPT2 and vibrational SCF go beyond the harmonic model using higher derivatives.
