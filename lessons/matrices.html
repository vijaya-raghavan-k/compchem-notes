Matrices and matrix–vector products

Goal: see a matrix as a machine that turns one vector into another, read its columns, and understand the Hessian as the matrix that turns a displacement into the change of force it causes.

Step 1 · See it

A matrix moves every vector

A 2×2 matrix \(\mathbf A\) takes any vector \(\mathbf v\) and produces a new vector \(\mathbf A\mathbf v\). Applied to every point at once, it stretches, rotates or shears the whole plane. The faint grid below shows where the original square grid ends up; the pink curve shows where the unit circle goes.

The most useful fact on this page: the blue arrow is the first column of the matrix and the green arrow is the second. A matrix is completely described by where it sends the two basis vectors \(\mathbf e_1 = (1, 0)\) and \(\mathbf e_2 = (0, 1)\). Everything else follows, because any \(\mathbf v = v_1\mathbf e_1 + v_2\mathbf e_2\) goes to \(v_1(\text{column 1}) + v_2(\text{column 2})\).

Step 2 · Calculate it

Matrix times vector, by hand

Take a symmetric matrix, the kind every Hessian is, and a displacement:

\[ \mathbf H = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Delta\mathbf x = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \]
  1. Row times column. Each output component is the dot product (lesson 1.2) of one row with the vector: \[ \mathbf H\Delta\mathbf x = \begin{pmatrix} 2\cdot 1 + 1\cdot(-1) \\ 1\cdot 1 + 3\cdot(-1) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} \]
  2. Or columns. The same result as a combination of columns: \(1\cdot\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix} + (-1)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\end{pmatrix}\).
  3. A number from a matrix. The quadratic energy of lesson 1.7 is a dot product of \(\Delta\mathbf x\) with \(\mathbf H\Delta\mathbf x\): \[ \tfrac12\,\Delta\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H\,\Delta\mathbf x = \tfrac12\,(1,\ -1)\cdot(1,\ -2) = \tfrac12\,(1 + 2) = 1.5 \] Here \(\Delta\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}\) (the transpose) is the same numbers written as a row.
  4. Symmetric means \(H_{ij} = H_{ji}\): the matrix equals its own transpose. For a Hessian this is the equality of mixed derivatives from lesson 1.6.
Key idea. \(\mathbf H\Delta\mathbf x\) is a vector (how the gradient changes); \(\Delta\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H\Delta\mathbf x\) is a number (twice the energy change of the quadratic model).

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

The Hessian as a force-constant matrix

For a molecule, the Hessian collects every second derivative of the energy with respect to the \(3N\) Cartesian coordinates:

\[ H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}, \qquad \mathbf H \text{ is } 3N \times 3N \]

Near a minimum the gradient is zero, and expanding it to first order gives a many-atom Hooke's law:

\[ \mathbf g(\mathbf x_0 + \Delta\mathbf x) \approx \mathbf H\,\Delta\mathbf x \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf F \approx -\mathbf H\,\Delta\mathbf x \]

So \(H_{ij}\) answers: if I move coordinate \(j\) by one unit, how much force appears on coordinate \(i\)? Column \(j\) is the full force pattern caused by moving coordinate \(j\) alone. Try it on a linear triatomic held together by two identical springs.

One column of the Hessian = one displacementChoose which atom to push

Push atom 1: it feels a pull back, atom 2 feels a pull forward, and atom 3 feels nothing, because no spring connects them. That is column 1 of the matrix. Now press "move all three": every force vanishes. Moving the whole molecule costs no energy, so every row of the Hessian adds up to zero. This is the matrix version of the zero-net-force rule from lesson 1.5.

How a numerical Hessian is built

The column picture is literally the algorithm. To compute column \(j\), displace coordinate \(j\) by \(\pm h\), compute analytical gradients at both geometries, and difference them:

\[ H_{ij} \approx \frac{g_i(\mathbf x_0 + h\,\mathbf e_j) - g_i(\mathbf x_0 - h\,\mathbf e_j)}{2h} \]

Repeating for all \(3N\) coordinates gives the full matrix from \(6N\) gradient calculations, which is why numerical frequency jobs print a long list of displaced geometries.

The structure of a molecular Hessian

For water the Hessian is 9 × 9. It splits naturally into 3 × 3 blocks, one for each pair of atoms. Diagonal blocks describe how an atom's own force changes when it moves; off-diagonal blocks describe how moving one atom pushes on another.

Going deeper: units, solving instead of inverting, and the secant condition

Units. Hessian elements are energy per length squared. Programs store Hartree/Bohr²; 1 Hartree/Bohr² = 1556.9 N/m = 15.569 mdyn/Å. A typical bond-stretch force constant of 5 mdyn/Å is therefore about 0.32 Hartree/Bohr².

Determinant. The determinant of a 2×2 matrix is the factor by which it scales areas (the unit square becomes a parallelogram of area \(\det\mathbf A\)). A zero determinant squashes the plane onto a line, and the matrix has no inverse. Because every row of a free molecule's Cartesian Hessian sums to zero, its determinant is exactly zero: it cannot be inverted until translations and rotations are removed.

Solving, not inverting. The Newton step \(\Delta\mathbf x = -\mathbf H^{-1}\mathbf g\) is computed by solving the linear system \(\mathbf H\Delta\mathbf x = -\mathbf g\), usually via a factorization or an eigen-decomposition. Forming \(\mathbf H^{-1}\) explicitly is slower and less accurate.

The secant condition. From \(\Delta\mathbf g \approx \mathbf H\Delta\mathbf x\), two successive optimization steps tell you how the Hessian acts along the step direction without computing it. Quasi-Newton methods such as BFGS update an approximate Hessian so that \(\mathbf H_\text{new}\Delta\mathbf x = \Delta\mathbf g\) holds exactly after every step (lesson 2.1).
