Derivatives and partial derivatives

Goal: understand a derivative as a local slope, compute it by finite differences and know their errors, and extend the idea to many coordinates with partial derivatives and the chain rule.

Step 1 · See it

Slope from two nearby points

The derivative \(E'(r)\) answers one question: if I change \(r\) a tiny bit, how fast does the energy change? Geometrically it is the slope of the tangent line. A computer cannot take "a tiny bit" literally, so it uses two points a distance \(h\) apart and draws a secant line through them. As \(h\) shrinks, the secant turns into the tangent.

Tangent and secant on a bond potentialDrag along the curve
E(r)tangent (exact slope)secant (estimate)

Where the curve bends strongly (short bond lengths), the forward secant drifts away from the tangent unless \(h\) is small. The central secant, which looks equally far left and right, stays close even for fairly large \(h\). The next figure shows why, and why \(h\) cannot simply be made tiny.

Step 2 · Calculate it

Two difference formulas, by hand

Take \(E(x) = x^3 - 2x\). Its exact derivative is \(E'(x) = 3x^2 - 2\), so at \(x = 1\) the slope is exactly 1. Now estimate it with \(h = 0.1\).

  1. Evaluate the function at the points needed: \[ E(1.1) = 1.331 - 2.2 = -0.869,\quad E(1) = -1,\quad E(0.9) = 0.729 - 1.8 = -1.071 \]
  2. Forward difference uses the point and one step ahead: \[ E'(1) \approx \frac{E(1.1) - E(1)}{0.1} = \frac{0.131}{0.1} = 1.31 \qquad (\text{error } 0.31) \]
  3. Central difference uses one step on each side: \[ E'(1) \approx \frac{E(1.1) - E(0.9)}{0.2} = \frac{0.202}{0.2} = 1.01 \qquad (\text{error } 0.01) \]
  4. Why 30 times better? Expand \(E(x\pm h)\) in powers of \(h\) (lesson 1.7 does this properly). The forward error is about \(\tfrac{h}{2}E''\), which shrinks like \(h\). In the central formula the \(E''\) terms from both sides cancel, leaving about \(\tfrac{h^2}{6}E'''\), which shrinks like \(h^2\). Check: \(\tfrac{0.1}{2}\cdot 6 = 0.3\) and \(\tfrac{0.01}{6}\cdot 6 = 0.01\).

Partial derivatives

When \(E\) depends on several coordinates, a partial derivative changes one coordinate and freezes all the others. It is the slope of one slice, exactly like the slice plot in lesson 1.3. For \(E(x, y) = x^2 y + 3y^2\):

\[ \frac{\partial E}{\partial x} = 2xy, \qquad \frac{\partial E}{\partial y} = x^2 + 6y \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \text{at } (1, 2):\ \ \frac{\partial E}{\partial x} = 4,\ \ \frac{\partial E}{\partial y} = 13 \]

To differentiate with respect to \(x\), treat \(y\) as a constant number, and vice versa.

The chain rule

A bond energy depends on the distance \(r\), but a program moves Cartesian coordinates. The chain rule connects the two: \(\dfrac{\partial E}{\partial x_1} = \dfrac{dE}{dr}\,\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x_1}\). For two atoms on the \(x\)-axis, \(r = x_2 - x_1\), so \(\partial r/\partial x_1 = -1\) and \(\partial r/\partial x_2 = +1\). If \(dE/dr = -50\) kcal/mol/Å (bond compressed, energy falls as it lengthens), then

\[ \frac{\partial E}{\partial x_1} = (-50)(-1) = +50, \qquad \frac{\partial E}{\partial x_2} = (-50)(+1) = -50\quad \text{kcal/mol/Å} \]

Equal and opposite, as they must be: moving both atoms together changes nothing.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

Numerical derivatives in real calculations

Programs compute derivatives in two ways. Analytical derivatives are derived on paper and coded; for DFT the full gradient costs roughly as much as one extra energy. Numerical derivatives displace coordinates and difference the results; they need no extra theory but many more calculations.

  • A numerical gradient by central differences needs 2 energies per coordinate: \(2 \times 3N = 6N\) single points. For 30 atoms that is 180 SCF calculations for one gradient.
  • Numerical frequencies (common when analytical second derivatives are unavailable for a method) difference analytical gradients: displace each of the \(3N\) coordinates forward and back, giving \(6N\) gradient calculations for one Hessian.
  • The step size is a compromise, shown below. Too large, and the formula's built-in (truncation) error dominates. Too small, and the tiny numerical noise in each energy is divided by a tiny \(h\) and blows up.
The step-size trade-offChange the noise in each energy
forward differencecentral differencecurrent h

This is why SCF convergence and integration grids are tightened for frequency calculations: they lower the noise floor, which lets the program use a smaller step and get cleaner second derivatives. You will see the consequence in lesson 1.11, as small spurious imaginary frequencies.

Gradient units

1 Hartree/Bohr = 1.88973 Hartree/Å = 51.422 eV/Å = 1185.8 kcal/mol/Å. Always check which one an output and a threshold use before comparing them.

Going deeper: how analytical gradients and the chain rule work in programs

Hellmann–Feynman. For an exact (or fully variational) wavefunction, \(\partial E/\partial x = \langle\Psi|\partial \hat H/\partial x|\Psi\rangle\): the force depends only on how the Hamiltonian changes, not on how the wavefunction changes. This is why analytic gradients are cheap.

Pulay forces. With atom-centred basis functions (Gaussians or Slater functions), the basis moves with the atoms, so the wavefunction's description changes even if the physics did not. That adds an extra "Pulay" term to the gradient. Plane-wave codes do not have it, because their basis does not move.

The Wilson B matrix. Optimizers often work in internal coordinates \(\mathbf q\) (bonds, angles, dihedrals). The chain rule written for all coordinates at once is a matrix of partial derivatives \(B_{ij} = \partial q_i/\partial x_j\), and Cartesian and internal gradients are related by \(\mathbf g_x = \mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\mathbf g_q\). The small two-atom example above is one row of this matrix.

Best step size. If each energy carries noise \(\varepsilon\), the central-difference error is roughly \(\tfrac{h^2}{6}|E'''| + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{h}\). Minimizing over \(h\) gives \(h^\ast \approx (3\varepsilon/|E'''|)^{1/3}\): the optimal step only shrinks as the cube root of the noise. Halving the noise improves the best achievable error by only a factor of about 1.6.
