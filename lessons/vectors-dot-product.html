Vector length, addition and the dot product

Goal: combine displacements, measure their size, and measure how well two directions agree. These three operations sit inside every convergence test, bond angle and mode analysis.

Step 1 · See it

Two arrows from the origin

In lesson 1.1 a vector was one move of one atom. Here we hold two vectors, \(\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf b\), and ask two questions: what happens if we make one move after the other, and how similar are their directions?

Vectors a and bDrag either arrow tip

Two things to try. In addition mode, notice that \(|\mathbf a + \mathbf b|\) is never longer than \(|\mathbf a| + |\mathbf b|\): equal only when the arrows point the same way. In dot product mode, rotate \(\mathbf b\) around \(\mathbf a\) and watch the sign of \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b\): positive when they lean together, zero at 90°, negative when they lean apart.

Step 2 · Calculate it

A worked example by hand

Take \(\mathbf a = (3,\ 1)\) and \(\mathbf b = (1,\ 2)\).

  1. Addition works component by component, exactly like subtraction in lesson 1.1: \[ \mathbf a + \mathbf b = \begin{pmatrix} 3+1 \\ 1+2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \]
  2. Length (also called the norm or magnitude) comes from Pythagoras: \[ |\mathbf a| = \sqrt{3^2+1^2} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.162, \qquad |\mathbf b| = \sqrt{1^2+2^2} = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.236 \]
  3. Dot product: multiply matching components and add. \[ \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = a_x b_x + a_y b_y = 3\cdot 1 + 1\cdot 2 = 5 \] The result is a single number, not a vector.
  4. Angle. The same dot product also equals \(|\mathbf a|\,|\mathbf b|\cos\theta\). Solving for the angle: \[ \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{|\mathbf a|\,|\mathbf b|} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{10}\,\sqrt{5}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{50}} = 0.7071 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta = 45^\circ \]
  5. Projection. How far does \(\mathbf b\) reach along the direction of \(\mathbf a\)? Divide \(\mathbf a\) by its length to get the unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf a}\), then dot: \[ \hat{\mathbf a} = \frac{\mathbf a}{|\mathbf a|} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.949 \\ 0.316 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf a}\cdot\mathbf b = \frac{5}{\sqrt{10}} \approx 1.581 \]
Key idea. The length of a vector is the square root of its dot product with itself: \(|\mathbf a| = \sqrt{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a}\). Lengths, angles and projections all come from one operation.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

Angles, convergence tests and mode overlaps

A bond angle from Cartesian coordinates

A program stores water as three positions (Å). Take

\[ \mathbf r_\mathrm{O} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0.117 \end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf r_{\mathrm{H}_1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0.757 \\ -0.469 \end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf r_{\mathrm{H}_2} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -0.757 \\ -0.469 \end{pmatrix} \]

The two bond vectors are displacements from oxygen: \(\mathbf u = \mathbf r_{\mathrm H_1} - \mathbf r_\mathrm O = (0,\ 0.757,\ -0.586)\) and \(\mathbf v = (0,\ -0.757,\ -0.586)\). Then

\[ \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{|\mathbf u|\,|\mathbf v|} = \frac{-0.5730 + 0.3434}{0.9573^2} = \frac{-0.2297}{0.9164} = -0.2506 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta = 104.5^\circ \]

This is how every visualizer and every internal-coordinate routine computes a bond angle: two displacement vectors and one dot product.

The two numbers in every optimization output

The gradient (lesson 1.5) is a vector \(\mathbf g\) with \(3N\) components. A program cannot test a whole vector against a threshold, so it reduces it to numbers. Two are standard:

\[ \text{max gradient} = \max_i |g_i|, \qquad \text{RMS gradient} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{3N}\sum_{i=1}^{3N} g_i^2} = \frac{|\mathbf g|}{\sqrt{3N}} \]
Two norms of one gradientEdit any component (Hartree/Å)

Try making one component large while keeping the rest small. The max norm reacts strongly; the RMS norm averages it away. This is why programs require both: RMS catches a generally poor geometry, max catches one atom that is still far off.

Overlap between two displacement patterns

The normalized dot product of two \(3N\)-component vectors, \(\cos\theta = \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b / (|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|)\), measures how similar two atomic motions are. A value near ±1 means the same motion (the sign only reflects which way the arrows were drawn), near 0 means unrelated motions. It is used to check whether a transition state's imaginary mode really moves the atoms the reaction needs, and to follow the "same" vibrational mode across related molecules.

Going deeper: inner products, norms and metrics

Cauchy–Schwarz. For any vectors, \(|\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b| \le |\mathbf a|\,|\mathbf b|\). This is why \(\cos\theta\) always lies between −1 and 1, and why the angle formula works in 30 or 3000 dimensions where no picture exists. The triangle inequality \(|\mathbf a+\mathbf b|\le|\mathbf a|+|\mathbf b|\) follows from it.

Different norms answer different questions. The ordinary length is the 2-norm \(\lVert\mathbf g\rVert_2\). The max gradient is the ∞-norm \(\lVert\mathbf g\rVert_\infty\). They are related by \(\lVert\mathbf g\rVert_\infty \le \lVert\mathbf g\rVert_2 \le \sqrt{3N}\,\lVert\mathbf g\rVert_\infty\), so for large molecules the same ∞-norm threshold allows a much larger total gradient.

Weighted inner products. Nothing forces every component to count equally. In vibrational analysis and IRC the natural choice is the mass-weighted inner product \[ \langle \mathbf a, \mathbf b\rangle_M = \sum_{i=1}^{3N} m_i\, a_i b_i \] Normal modes are orthogonal in this metric, not in the plain Cartesian one. Lesson 1.10 shows why.

Orthonormal bases. Unit vectors that are mutually perpendicular form a basis in which every vector's components are simply dot products: \(a_k = \hat{\mathbf e}_k\cdot\mathbf a\). Programs build such bases (by Gram–Schmidt) for the three translations and three rotations of a molecule, then remove those components from a gradient or Hessian. That is the projection step you see mentioned in frequency outputs.
