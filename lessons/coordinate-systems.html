Moving on the potential energy surface · 2.3

Coordinate systems

Goal: see why the same energy surface is a curved valley in Cartesian coordinates and a simple bowl in bonds and angles, learn how programs convert between the two with the Wilson B matrix, and know when internal coordinates fail.

Step 1 · See it

One surface, two descriptions

Hold the oxygen and one hydrogen of water fixed and move only the second hydrogen in the molecular plane. Its energy depends on the O–H distance \(r\) (a stiff stretch) and the H–O–H angle \(\theta\) (a soft bend). Plotted against the hydrogen's Cartesian position, the minimum is a narrow curved valley: the circle of constant bond length. Plotted against \(r\) and \(\theta\), the same energy is a plain elliptical bowl.

All three optimizers reach the same structure, and each path is drawn in both maps. The Cartesian optimizer has to learn that its valley curves; in internal coordinates there is nothing to learn, and with a sensible starting guess for the two force constants it finishes in a handful of steps. In a real molecule with dozens of coupled bends and torsions, each adding its own curvature, the advantage of internal coordinates grows accordingly.

Step 2 · Calculate it

Converting gradients and steps

The quantum chemistry program delivers Cartesian gradients. An internal-coordinate optimizer needs them in internal coordinates, and its steps must be turned back into Cartesian positions. Both conversions use the Wilson B matrix from lesson 1.4, \(B_{ij} = \partial q_i/\partial x_j\).

  1. Put the moving hydrogen at \((x, y) = (0.6,\ 0.8)\) with oxygen at the origin and the other hydrogen along \(+x\). Then \(r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2} = 1.0\) and \(\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x) = 53.13^\circ\).
  2. Differentiate \(r\) and \(\theta\) with respect to \(x\) and \(y\): \[ \mathbf B = \begin{pmatrix} \partial r/\partial x & \partial r/\partial y \\ \partial\theta/\partial x & \partial\theta/\partial y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x/r & y/r \\ -y/r^2 & x/r^2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 & 0.8 \\ -0.8 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix} \]
  3. The chain rule connects the gradients: \(\mathbf g_x = \mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\mathbf g_q\). With \(\mathbf g_q = (\partial E/\partial r,\ \partial E/\partial\theta) = (0.04,\ 0.10)\): \[ \mathbf g_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 & -0.8 \\ 0.8 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0.04 \\ 0.10 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.056 \\ 0.092 \end{pmatrix} \] The optimizer runs this backwards: from \(\mathbf g_x\) it solves for \(\mathbf g_q\).
  4. Back-transformation. The optimizer proposes an internal step, say \(\Delta\mathbf q = (0,\ 0.1\ \text{rad})\): open the angle, keep the bond. To first order \(\Delta\mathbf x = \mathbf B^{-1}\Delta\mathbf q = (-0.08,\ 0.06)\), giving \((0.52,\ 0.86)\). But now \(r = \sqrt{0.52^2 + 0.86^2} = 1.005\), not 1.000: a straight Cartesian step cannot exactly follow a circle. Programs therefore iterate the back-transformation until the internal coordinates match.
Key idea. Internal coordinates are curvilinear: they bend along the molecule's natural motions. That makes the energy nearly quadratic and separable in them, at the price of a nonlinear, iterative conversion back to Cartesians.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

Which coordinates programs use

  • Z-matrix. Each atom is placed by one bond, one angle and one dihedral relative to earlier atoms: exactly \(3N - 6\) coordinates. Intuitive and good for scans, but the choice of reference atoms can couple coordinates badly, and rings are awkward.
  • Redundant internal coordinates. Use every bond, every angle and every dihedral. For benzene that is far more than \(3N - 6 = 30\) coordinates. The redundancy is handled with a generalized inverse. This is the default in many molecular codes because no choice of reference atoms is needed.
  • Delocalized internal coordinates. Take the redundant set and form exactly \(3N - 6\) independent combinations of it, using the eigenvectors of \(\mathbf G = \mathbf B\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\) with nonzero eigenvalues (lesson 1.9 again). AMS can use delocalized coordinates for molecular optimizations; its geometry-optimization settings let you choose the coordinate type.
  • Cartesian coordinates. No conversion and no singularities. Preferred for very large systems, for periodic cells (together with the lattice vectors), and as a fallback when internal coordinates break down. Molecular dynamics always uses Cartesians.

Where internal coordinates struggle

  • Nearly linear angles (allenes, alkynes, linear transition states): near 180° the angle's derivative is ill-defined, and a dihedral through a linear angle is undefined. Programs add special linear-bend coordinates; if the output warns about failed back-transformation, switching to Cartesians usually works.
  • Weakly bound complexes: if two fragments share no bond, the automatically generated coordinates may not describe their relative position well. Adding intermolecular distances (or using Cartesians) helps.
  • Constraints and scans are where internals shine: freezing a dihedral or stepping a bond length (lesson 1.3's relaxed scan) is a matter of removing one coordinate from the optimization.
Going deeper: generalized inverses, Hessian transformation and model Hessians

Redundant gradients. With more internal coordinates than degrees of freedom, \(\mathbf B\) is rectangular and \(\mathbf G = \mathbf B\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\) is singular. The internal gradient is \(\mathbf g_q = \mathbf G^{-}\mathbf B\,\mathbf g_x\), with \(\mathbf G^{-}\) the generalized inverse built from the nonzero eigenvalues of \(\mathbf G\). The projector \(\mathbf P = \mathbf G\mathbf G^{-}\) removes step components that would change coordinates in impossible, inconsistent ways.

Hessians do not transform simply. \(\mathbf H_x = \mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H_q\mathbf B + \sum_i g_{q,i}\,\partial^2 q_i/\partial\mathbf x^2\). The second term, involving second derivatives of the coordinates, vanishes only at a stationary point. Frequencies are therefore coordinate-independent only at a converged geometry.

Model Hessians. Force constants are much more transferable in internal coordinates: every C–H stretch is stiff, every methyl torsion soft. Model Hessians (Lindh, Swart and others) assign each bond, angle and dihedral a force constant from atom types and distances. In the figure above, the "model Hessian" path uses exactly such a guess.

Connection to vibrations. \(\mathbf G\) with masses included, \(\mathbf G = \mathbf B\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\), is Wilson's kinetic matrix from the GF method (lesson 1.10). Optimization and vibrational analysis use the same coordinate machinery.
