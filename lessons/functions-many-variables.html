Functions of many variables

Goal: read the energy as one number computed from all atomic coordinates, \(E(\mathbf R)\), and understand what a potential energy surface is, how many dimensions it has, and why we can never draw it completely.

Step 1 · See it

Every geometry is a point; every point has an energy

A function takes inputs and returns one output. For a molecule, the inputs are the coordinates of its atoms and the output is the energy. To draw it we must pick at most two inputs. For water, choose the O–H bond length \(r\) (both bonds equal) and the H–O–H angle \(\theta\). Each point of the map below is one geometry; the shading and contour lines show its energy.

Model energy in kcal/mol relative to the minimum: Morse bond stretches plus a harmonic bend. The shape is realistic; the numbers are illustrative.

Three things to notice. The lowest point is the equilibrium geometry. Contour lines are crowded where the energy changes quickly (compressing the bond) and spread out where it changes slowly (stretching it). And the right-hand plot is just one horizontal line through the map: a function of one variable is a slice of a function of many.

Step 2 · Calculate it

Evaluating an energy by hand

The bond part of the model is a Morse function, a standard description of a chemical bond:

\[ E(r) = D_e\left(1 - e^{-a(r - r_e)}\right)^2, \qquad D_e = 120\ \text{kcal/mol},\ a = 2.2\ \text{Å}^{-1},\ r_e = 0.958\ \text{Å} \]
  1. Stretch the bond by 0.1 Å, to \(r = 1.058\) Å. Then \(a(r-r_e) = 2.2 \times 0.1 = 0.22\) and \(e^{-0.22} = 0.8025\). \[ E = 120\,(1 - 0.8025)^2 = 120 \times 0.0390 = 4.68\ \text{kcal/mol} \]
  2. Compress it by 0.1 Å, to \(r = 0.858\) Å. Now \(a(r-r_e) = -0.22\) and \(e^{+0.22} = 1.2461\). \[ E = 120\,(1 - 1.2461)^2 = 120 \times 0.0606 = 7.27\ \text{kcal/mol} \]
  3. The same 0.1 Å costs 55% more energy in compression. A real bond is not symmetric around its minimum; this asymmetry is called anharmonicity and returns in lesson 1.7.
  4. How many inputs does the full function need? Water has 3 atoms, so 9 Cartesian coordinates. But moving the whole molecule (3 translations) or turning it (3 rotations) cannot change its energy. That leaves \(9 - 6 = 3\) coordinates that matter: \(r_1\), \(r_2\) and \(\theta\). The map above shows a 2D cut through this 3D function, taken along \(r_1 = r_2\).
Key idea. For \(N\) atoms, \(E\) depends on \(3N - 6\) internal coordinates (\(3N - 5\) if the molecule is linear, because rotating a line about its own axis moves nothing). This number is the dimension of the potential energy surface.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

What programs actually do with E(R)

Write all \(3N\) Cartesian coordinates as one column \(\mathbf R\), as in lesson 1.1. The quantum chemistry program is then a machine that evaluates one function:

\[ \mathbf R = (x_1, y_1, z_1, \dots, x_N, y_N, z_N) \ \longmapsto\ E(\mathbf R) \]
  • Single-point calculation: one evaluation of \(E\) at one \(\mathbf R\). For DFT this means solving the SCF equations once.
  • Rigid scan: change one coordinate in steps, keep all others fixed, evaluate \(E\) each time. This is a straight-line slice like the right-hand plot above.
  • Relaxed scan: change one coordinate in steps, but optimize all other coordinates at every step. This follows the valley floor instead of a straight line, and is the right way to estimate a barrier along a chosen coordinate.
  • Geometry optimization: find the lowest point nearby without mapping anything. How that is possible without a map is the subject of lessons 1.4 to 1.7.

Why mapping is impossible

With only 10 points per coordinate, the number of single points needed to map a surface grows explosively:

Going deeper: what E(R) really is

Born–Oppenheimer. Nuclei are thousands of times heavier than electrons, so for a fixed nuclear arrangement \(\mathbf R\) we solve the electronic problem alone. Its lowest eigenvalue plus the nuclear repulsion is \(E(\mathbf R)\). The surface exists because of this separation; lesson 3.1 returns to it.

One surface per electronic state. The ground state \(S_0\), the lowest triplet \(T_1\) and the excited states each have their own \(E(\mathbf R)\). Surfaces can approach or cross, and radical or photochemical reactions may switch between them. For closed-shell thermal reactions we usually stay on \(S_0\).

Invariance in symbols. For any translation \(\mathbf t\) and rotation \(\mathbf Q\), \(E(\mathbf R + \mathbf t) = E(\mathbf R)\) and \(E(\mathbf Q\mathbf R) = E(\mathbf R)\). These six exact symmetries are why six eigenvalues of the Hessian come out (nearly) zero in every frequency calculation, a fact we will use in lessons 1.9 and 1.10.

Coordinates describe, they do not change. The energy is one function of the geometry. Writing it as \(E(x_1,\dots,z_N)\) or \(E(r_1, r_2, \theta)\) is a choice of description. The choice does not change minima or barrier heights, but it strongly changes how easily an optimizer finds them (lesson 2.3).

Smoothness is approximate. Calculus assumes \(E\) is smooth. In practice, finite integration grids and incomplete SCF convergence add tiny noise to \(E(\mathbf R)\). It is invisible in energies but can matter for second derivatives (lesson 1.11).
