Imaginary frequencies and transition states

Goal: understand why a negative Hessian eigenvalue becomes an imaginary frequency, verify a transition state properly, and tell a real reaction mode from numerical noise.

Step 1 · See it

The shaded band is where one curvature is negative.

Also notice: inside the shaded band, points that are not stationary still have a negative curvature, but their gradient is not zero, so they are not transition states.

Step 2 · Calculate it

Why the frequency becomes imaginary

  1. From lesson 1.10, each mode satisfies \(\mathbf H_\text{mw}\mathbf v = \omega^2\mathbf v\), so \(\omega = \sqrt{\lambda}\). Along a direction of negative curvature, \(\lambda < 0\), and the square root is imaginary: \[ \lambda < 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega = \sqrt{\lambda} = i\sqrt{|\lambda|} \]
  2. What does that mean physically? Solve the equation of motion along that mode, \(\ddot q = -\lambda q = +|\lambda|\,q\): \[ q(t) = A\,e^{+\sqrt{|\lambda|}\,t} + B\,e^{-\sqrt{|\lambda|}\,t} \] No oscillation: a small push grows exponentially. The structure is unstable along this one direction, like a ball balanced on a ridge.
  3. Programs still report a number by applying the usual conversion to \(\sqrt{|\lambda|}\) and marking it, either with an \(i\) or, more commonly, with a minus sign. For \(\lambda = -0.088\) Hartree/(Bohr² amu): \[ \tilde\nu = 5140.48\,\sqrt{0.088} = 1525 \ \Rightarrow\ \text{printed as } 1525i \text{ or } -1525\ \text{cm}^{-1} \] The minus sign is a label, not a negative frequency.
  4. How small is a small imaginary mode? For \(-20\) cm⁻¹, \(|\lambda| = (20/5140.48)^2 = 1.5\times10^{-5}\) Hartree/(Bohr² amu), about 6000 times smaller than the reaction mode above. Curvatures that small are comparable to the numerical noise of DFT gradients (lesson 1.4).
Key idea. An imaginary frequency is a negative Hessian eigenvalue after mass weighting. One of them at a stationary point marks a first-order saddle; its eigenvector is the motion that turns reactant into product.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

Verifying a transition state, and handling spurious modes

A transition-state checklist

  1. Stationary. The optimization converged on all gradient and step criteria. A negative eigenvalue at a non-stationary point (anywhere in the shaded band above) means nothing.
  2. Exactly one imaginary frequency. Zero means you found a minimum; two or more means a higher-order saddle, and the extra imaginary modes show directions in which the structure can still relax.
  3. The right motion. Animate the imaginary mode. It should move the atoms that the reaction moves: the bonds being formed and broken. A methyl rotation as the imaginary mode means you found a conformational saddle, not your reaction.
  4. The right connection. Run an IRC (lesson 2.5) in both directions and confirm it reaches the intended reactant and product, as the steepest-descent paths did in Step 1.
  5. A plausible magnitude. The size of the imaginary frequency reflects how sharply the barrier top curves along the reaction path, scaled by the moving masses. Hydrogen transfers typically give large values (often above 1000i cm⁻¹), heavy-atom rearrangements smaller ones.
Bridge to my own work: β-H transfer. In a concerted six-membered β-hydrogen transfer (the classic cis-elimination of esters), the imaginary mode should show the β-hydrogen moving from carbon toward the carbonyl oxygen, coupled with the alkyl C–O bond lengthening. In an AMS frequency output, look for the single negative entry in the frequency list, open its normal-mode displacements, and check that the largest components belong to that hydrogen and those two bonds. Then confirm with an IRC.

Small imaginary frequencies at a "minimum"

A value such as −15 or −40 cm⁻¹ on a structure that should be a minimum is common, especially for floppy molecules with methyl rotors or long chains. Typical causes and fixes:

  • Loose geometry convergence. The structure is slightly off the stationary point. Re-optimize with tighter criteria.
  • Numerical noise. Coarse integration grids or loose SCF convergence add noise to gradients, which a numerical Hessian amplifies (lesson 1.4). Use a finer grid and tighter SCF for the frequency job.
  • A real shallow saddle. Displace the geometry along the imaginary mode (a small fraction of the printed displacement, in either direction), re-optimize, and recompute frequencies. If the mode disappears and the energy drops, the original structure really was a saddle.
  • Imposed symmetry. A structure forced to be planar or symmetric may be a saddle in the full space. Lower or remove the symmetry and repeat.

The imaginary frequency in kinetics

Transition state theory (lesson 2.7) treats the imaginary mode as the reaction coordinate and leaves it out of the partition function. Its magnitude enters tunneling corrections. The simplest is Wigner's,

\[ \kappa = 1 + \frac{1}{24}\left(\frac{h c\,|\tilde\nu^\ddagger|}{k_B T}\right)^2 \]

For \(|\tilde\nu^\ddagger| = 1500\) cm⁻¹ at 600 K, \(hc\tilde\nu/k_BT = 1500 \times 1.4388/600 = 3.60\) and \(\kappa = 1 + 3.60^2/24 = 1.54\). At 298 K the same formula gives 3.2, a value large enough that more rigorous tunneling methods are needed.

Going deeper: why exactly one, and what can still go wrong

Murrell–Laidler. The minimum-energy path between two minima crosses its highest point at a saddle with exactly one negative curvature. If the highest point had two, a path around it would be lower, so it could not be the highest point of the lowest path. This is why the transition state is defined as a first-order saddle.

Higher-order saddles and symmetry. Symmetric structures are often second-order saddles: one imaginary mode is the reaction, another breaks the symmetry. Following the second mode down usually leads to a lower, less symmetric transition state.

One saddle is not the whole story. Flexible molecules have many transition-state conformers. The relevant barrier comes from the lowest of them (or a Boltzmann-weighted set), so conformer searches matter at transition states as much as at minima. At high temperature the free-energy maximum along the path can shift away from the energy saddle, which variational transition state theory accounts for.

Back to the start. In \(\mathbf H\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v\): \(\mathbf H\) is the matrix of second derivatives of \(E(\mathbf R)\), built column by column from gradient differences; \(\mathbf v\) is a \(3N\)-component displacement of all atoms, the same kind of vector as in lesson 1.1; \(\lambda\) is the curvature along it, which after mass weighting is \(\omega^2\). A negative \(\lambda\) at a stationary point is the reaction coordinate. Every symbol now has a physical, mathematical and computational meaning.
