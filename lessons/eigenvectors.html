Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Goal: understand \(\mathbf H\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v\) as the special directions a matrix only stretches, find them by hand for a 2×2 case, and use them to read curvature, classify stationary points and decouple coordinates.

Step 1 · See it

Directions that do not turn

In lesson 1.8 a matrix turned almost every vector into a vector pointing somewhere else. For a symmetric matrix there are always special directions where \(\mathbf H\mathbf v\) points exactly along \(\mathbf v\) (or exactly opposite). Along those directions the matrix acts like a single number \(\lambda\): it only stretches, shrinks or flips. Those directions are the eigenvectors; the stretch factors are the eigenvalues.

Step 2 · Calculate it

Finding eigenvalues and eigenvectors by hand

Take \(\mathbf H = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\). We want \(\mathbf H\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v\) with \(\mathbf v \ne \mathbf 0\).

  1. Rewrite as \((\mathbf H - \lambda\mathbf I)\mathbf v = \mathbf 0\), where \(\mathbf I\) is the identity matrix. A nonzero \(\mathbf v\) can only be squashed to zero if the matrix \(\mathbf H - \lambda\mathbf I\) flattens the plane, which means its determinant is zero: \[ \det\begin{pmatrix} 2-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 2-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = 0 \]
  2. Solve: \(2 - \lambda = \pm 1\), so \(\lambda_1 = 1\) and \(\lambda_2 = 3\).
  3. For each eigenvalue, solve for the direction. For \(\lambda = 3\): \((2-3)v_x + v_y = 0\), so \(v_y = v_x\), giving \(\mathbf v_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,\ 1)\). For \(\lambda = 1\): \(v_y = -v_x\), giving \(\mathbf v_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,\ -1)\).
  4. Check: \(\mathbf H(1, 1) = (2+1,\ 1+2) = (3, 3) = 3\,(1, 1)\). ✓ And \(\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_2 = \tfrac12(1 - 1) = 0\): perpendicular, as promised.
  5. Two shortcuts that always hold: the eigenvalues add up to the diagonal sum (the trace, \(2 + 2 = 4 = 1 + 3\)) and multiply to the determinant (\(4 - 1 = 3 = 1\times 3\)).

Diagonalization: the coupling disappears

Put the unit eigenvectors as the columns of a matrix \(\mathbf U\) and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of \(\mathbf\Lambda\). Then

\[ \mathbf H = \mathbf U\mathbf\Lambda\mathbf U^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \mathbf\Lambda = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{pmatrix} \]

Measure positions along the eigenvectors instead of along \(x\) and \(y\): \(\mathbf q = \mathbf U^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x\). The quadratic energy of lesson 1.7 becomes a sum of independent pieces, with no cross term:

\[ E = \tfrac12\,\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H\,\mathbf x = \tfrac12\left(\lambda_1 q_1^2 + \lambda_2 q_2^2\right) \]

This is the single most important idea for vibrations. In eigenvector coordinates, coupled motions separate into independent one-dimensional problems, each with its own curvature \(\lambda_k\).

The hidden saddle, solved

Lesson 1.6 had \(E = x^2 + 4xy + y^2\), whose Hessian is \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\). Its eigenvalues are \(2 \pm 4\): \(\lambda = 6\) along \((1, 1)\) and \(\lambda = -2\) along \((1, -1)\). The negative eigenvalue reveals the downhill direction \(y = -x\) that the axis curvatures hid.

Key idea. Eigenvalues of the Hessian are the curvatures along its principal directions. Their signs, not the signs of the diagonal elements, decide whether a point is a minimum or a saddle.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

Reading a Hessian through its eigenvalues

The matrix from Step 1 also defines a surface, \(E = \tfrac12\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H\mathbf x\). Its eigenvectors are the axes of the contour ellipses, and its eigenvalues are the curvatures along them. Use the same sliders above; this map follows them.

The same matrix as an energy surfaceControlled by the sliders in Step 1
  • Index of a stationary point. Count the negative eigenvalues of the Hessian. Zero: a minimum. Exactly one: a first-order saddle point, the mathematical definition of a transition state. Two or more: a higher-order saddle, which is not a transition state for any single reaction.
  • Six eigenvalues near zero. A molecule's \(3N\times3N\) Cartesian Hessian always has 6 eigenvalues of (almost) zero, 5 for linear molecules. Their eigenvectors are the translations and rotations, along which the energy does not change. The remaining \(3N - 6\) eigenvalues describe the internal motions. Numerical noise makes the "zeros" slightly positive or negative, which is why programs project these motions out explicitly.
  • Transition-state searches. Eigenvector-following methods compute the Hessian's eigenvectors and then step uphill along the one with the lowest eigenvalue while stepping downhill along all others (lesson 2.4).
  • Conditioning. The ratio of the largest to smallest positive eigenvalue sets how slowly simple optimizers converge (lesson 1.5). Stiff bonds plus floppy torsions give ratios of 10³ or more.
Going deeper: the spectral theorem, Rayleigh quotients and where else eigenproblems appear

Spectral theorem. Every real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and a full set of mutually perpendicular eigenvectors. This is why Hessians, which are symmetric, can always be diagonalized by a rotation \(\mathbf U\) and why their curvatures are real numbers. A non-symmetric matrix can have complex eigenvalues and non-perpendicular eigenvectors; the rotation preset in lesson 1.8 has no real eigenvector at all.

Rayleigh quotient. For any direction, \(R(\mathbf v) = \mathbf v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H\mathbf v/\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf v\) is the curvature along \(\mathbf v\), and \(\lambda_\min \le R(\mathbf v) \le \lambda_\max\), as the lower plot in Step 1 shows. The dimer method for transition states finds the lowest-curvature direction by minimizing \(R\) using only gradients, without ever building \(\mathbf H\).

How computers do it. The characteristic polynomial is never used for large matrices. Standard routines reduce \(\mathbf H\) to tridiagonal form (Householder) and then apply QR iterations, costing order \(n^3\). When only a few eigenpairs are needed from a huge matrix (lowest Hessian modes, excited states in TD-DFT), iterative Davidson or Lanczos methods use only matrix–vector products.

The same equation everywhere. \(\hat H\psi = E\psi\) is an eigenvalue problem for the electronic Hamiltonian. The SCF equations \(\mathbf F\mathbf C = \mathbf S\mathbf C\boldsymbol\varepsilon\) are a generalized eigenvalue problem whose eigenvalues are orbital energies (lesson 3.3). In QTAIM the eigenvalues of the Hessian of the electron density classify critical points: a bond critical point has two negative eigenvalues and one positive (lesson 4.2). Learning this one idea well pays off across the whole field.
