Gradients and forces

Goal: collect all partial derivatives into one vector, see that it points straight uphill and perpendicular to contour lines, and understand why the force on the atoms is \(\mathbf F = -\nabla E\).

Step 1 · See it

The arrow that points uphill

At any point of a surface you can compute one partial derivative per coordinate. Stacked into a column, they form the gradient \(\nabla E\). It is a vector, so it has a direction and a length, and both have a meaning: it points in the direction of steepest increase, and its length is how steep that is. The force is the same arrow reversed.

gradient ∇E (uphill)force −∇E (downhill)contour direction

Move the point around and check three facts. The arrows are always perpendicular to the contour line through the point. They are long where contours are crowded and short where they are sparse. And at the bottom of each well and at the pass between them, the arrows shrink to nothing: the gradient is zero at every stationary point.

Step 2 · Calculate it

Gradient, force and one downhill step

Take the simple bowl \(E(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2\) at the point \((1,\ 1)\).

  1. Partial derivatives (lesson 1.4), stacked into a column: \[ \nabla E = \begin{pmatrix} \partial E/\partial x \\ \partial E/\partial y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2x \\ 6y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \]
  2. Its length is the steepest slope available from this point: \[ |\nabla E| = \sqrt{2^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{40} \approx 6.32 \]
  3. The directional derivative, the slope along any unit direction \(\hat{\mathbf u}\), is a dot product (lesson 1.2): \(D_{\hat{\mathbf u}}E = \nabla E\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}\). Along \(x\), \(\hat{\mathbf u} = (1, 0)\), the slope is 2. Since \(\nabla E\cdot\hat{\mathbf u} = |\nabla E|\cos\theta\), it is largest (6.32) when \(\hat{\mathbf u}\) points along the gradient, and zero when \(\hat{\mathbf u}\) is perpendicular to it. Zero slope means walking along a contour line: that is why the gradient is perpendicular to contours.
  4. The force is the negative gradient: \(\mathbf F = -\nabla E = (-2,\ -6)\). It points straight downhill.
  5. One steepest-descent step moves along the force with a step factor \(\alpha = 0.1\): \[ \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}_{\text{new}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + 0.1\begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ -6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.8 \\ 0.4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad E: 4.00 \to 0.64 + 0.48 = 1.12 \] A second step gives \((0.64,\ 0.16)\) and \(E = 0.49\). This is the simplest possible optimizer.
Key idea. The gradient tells you which way is uphill and how steep it is, using only first derivatives at the current point. No map of the surface is needed.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

Forces in optimizations and dynamics

For a molecule the gradient has \(3N\) components, three per atom. Reading it three at a time gives the force on each atom:

\[ \mathbf F_A = -\begin{pmatrix} \partial E/\partial x_A \\ \partial E/\partial y_A \\ \partial E/\partial z_A \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A = 1, \dots, N \]
  • Geometry optimization moves atoms along their forces (with cleverer step choices, lesson 2.1) until every force is close to zero. The convergence test reports the max and RMS of exactly this vector.
  • Molecular dynamics feeds the same forces into Newton's law, \(m_A\ddot{\mathbf r}_A = \mathbf F_A\), to move atoms in time.
  • A free sanity check. Moving the whole molecule cannot change its energy, so the forces must add up to zero: \(\sum_A \mathbf F_A = \mathbf 0\) (and likewise for torques). A clearly nonzero sum in an output, without an external field, points to an inconsistent or poorly converged calculation.

Steepest descent, step by step

Run the simplest optimizer on the surface from Step 1. Each step moves by \(\alpha\) times the force.

Steepest descentDrag to choose the start

Increase \(\alpha\) and the path starts to zig-zag across the valley. The gradient points across the narrow valley much more than along it, so a long step overshoots to the opposite wall. Too large an \(\alpha\) and it never settles. This weakness is why real optimizers also use second-derivative information (lessons 1.7 and 2.1).

Comparing convergence thresholds

Programs state gradient thresholds in different units. Gaussian's default maximum force is \(4.5\times10^{-4}\) Hartree/Bohr, which is \(4.5\times10^{-4}\times 1.8897 = 8.5\times10^{-4}\) Hartree/Å. AMS states its gradient criterion in Hartree/Å (its documented default is \(10^{-3}\)). Convert before comparing, and check your version's documentation for the exact definitions.

Going deeper: why the gradient behaves this way

Linear approximation. Near a point \(\mathbf x\), \(E(\mathbf x + \Delta\mathbf x) \approx E(\mathbf x) + \nabla E\cdot\Delta\mathbf x\). The gradient is the vector that turns any small displacement into the energy change it causes. Lesson 1.7 extends this to second order.

Zero net force, derived. Translational invariance means \(E(\mathbf R + \mathbf t) = E(\mathbf R)\) for a uniform shift \(\mathbf t\) of all atoms. Differentiating with respect to \(t_x\) at \(\mathbf t = 0\) gives \(\sum_A \partial E/\partial x_A = 0\). The same argument with rotations gives zero net torque. These 6 conditions are why the gradient effectively lives in \(3N-6\) dimensions.

"Steepest" depends on how you measure distance. Steepest descent assumes every coordinate counts equally. If distances are measured with masses (\(\sqrt{m}\,\Delta x\)), the steepest-descent path changes. The intrinsic reaction coordinate is precisely the steepest-descent path in mass-weighted coordinates (lesson 2.5).

Speed of steepest descent. Near a minimum with largest and smallest curvatures \(\lambda_\max\) and \(\lambda_\min\), the best fixed-step steepest descent reduces the error by a factor of about \((\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)\) per step, where \(\kappa = \lambda_\max/\lambda_\min\) is the condition number. Floppy molecules have tiny \(\lambda_\min\) (soft torsions next to stiff bonds), so \(\kappa\) is huge and steepest descent crawls. Curvatures as eigenvalues are the subject of lesson 1.9.
