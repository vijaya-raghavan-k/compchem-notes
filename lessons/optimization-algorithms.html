Moving on the potential energy surface · 2.1

Geometry optimization algorithms

Goal: understand how steepest descent, conjugate gradient, Newton and quasi-Newton (BFGS) methods choose their steps, why they differ so much in speed, and which one your program is running.

Step 1 · See it

Four optimizers, one curved valley

Real potential energy surfaces have long, curved valleys: a soft coordinate (an angle, a torsion) coupled to a stiff one (a bond). Below is a model surface of exactly that shape, with its minimum at (1, 1). All four optimizers start from the same point and stop when the gradient length falls below \(10^{-4}\).

Optimizer race on a curved valleyTap to choose the starting point

"Gradients" counts every gradient evaluation, including the extra ones a line search needs. For a DFT calculation each one is a full SCF plus gradient, so this column is the real cost.

Steepest descent zig-zags down the valley walls and does not finish within 500 steps. Conjugate gradient follows the valley much better. Newton, using the exact Hessian, takes large well-aimed steps. BFGS, which never computes a Hessian, comes close to Newton after a few steps because it learns the curvature from the gradients it has already seen.

Step 2 · Calculate it

Learning curvature from gradients

The idea behind quasi-Newton methods fits in one dimension. Newton's step needs \(E''\) (lesson 1.7). If we do not have it, estimate it from the last two gradients, a finite difference made of points we already paid for:

\[ E''(x_k) \approx \frac{g_k - g_{k-1}}{x_k - x_{k-1}}, \qquad x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{g_k}{E''(x_k)} \]
  1. Take \(E = x^4 - 2x^2\), so \(g = 4x^3 - 4x\), with two starting points \(x_0 = 1.5\) (\(g = 7.500\)) and \(x_1 = 1.3\) (\(g = 3.588\)).
  2. Curvature estimate: \(\dfrac{3.588 - 7.500}{1.3 - 1.5} = \dfrac{-3.912}{-0.2} = 19.56\). The true value at 1.3 is \(12(1.3)^2 - 4 = 16.28\): not exact, but in the right range.
  3. Step: \(x_2 = 1.3 - 3.588/19.56 = 1.1166\).
  4. Continue, each time using the two latest gradients:

  5. The curvature estimate converges to the true value 8 as the steps converge to the minimum. Convergence is slightly slower than Newton (lesson 1.7) but needs no second derivatives at all.

In many dimensions the same idea is the secant condition from lesson 1.8: after a step \(\mathbf s = \mathbf x_{k+1} - \mathbf x_k\) with gradient change \(\mathbf y = \mathbf g_{k+1} - \mathbf g_k\), the updated Hessian must satisfy \(\mathbf B_{k+1}\mathbf s = \mathbf y\). BFGS is the particular update that does this while keeping \(\mathbf B\) symmetric and positive definite:

\[ \mathbf B_{k+1} = \mathbf B_k + \frac{\mathbf y\mathbf y^{\mathsf T}}{\mathbf y^{\mathsf T}\mathbf s} - \frac{\mathbf B_k\mathbf s\,\mathbf s^{\mathsf T}\mathbf B_k}{\mathbf s^{\mathsf T}\mathbf B_k\mathbf s} \]
Key idea. Every step reveals how the gradient changes along one direction. Quasi-Newton methods store that information as an approximate Hessian, so they become Newton-like without ever computing second derivatives.

  • Starting Hessian matters. BFGS needs an initial guess. A unit matrix works but wastes early steps. Programs use a model Hessian built from bonds, angles and torsions with typical force constants (for example Lindh or Swart models), which often halves the number of steps. For stubborn cases you can compute the exact Hessian once at the start.
  • What programs run. Molecular DFT codes default to quasi-Newton optimizers in internal coordinates. In AMS you can choose the method (quasi-Newton, L-BFGS, FIRE, conjugate gradients) in the geometry-optimization settings; Gaussian's default "Berny" optimizer combines a quasi-Newton update with rational function steps. Check your version's documentation for the exact defaults.
  • Cost is gradients. An optimization that needs 40 instead of 20 steps costs twice the CPU time, because every step is a full SCF and gradient. A better optimizer setup is often the cheapest speed-up available.
  • Signs of trouble. Energy rising for several steps, steps repeatedly hitting the maximum step size, or oscillating gradients usually mean the Hessian model is poor, the SCF is not converging consistently, or the structure is near a saddle. Restarting with a computed Hessian often fixes it.
Going deeper: line searches, positive definiteness and rational function steps

Line searches and the Wolfe conditions. Methods that pick a direction then a step length require the step to lower the energy enough (Armijo condition, \(E(\mathbf x+\alpha\mathbf d) \le E(\mathbf x) + c_1\alpha\,\mathbf g^{\mathsf T}\mathbf d\)) and to flatten the slope enough (curvature condition). Satisfying both guarantees \(\mathbf y^{\mathsf T}\mathbf s > 0\), which keeps the BFGS Hessian positive definite and every step a descent direction.

Conjugacy. On an exact quadratic in \(n\) dimensions, conjugate gradient with exact line searches converges in at most \(n\) steps: each new direction is chosen so that minimizing along it does not undo progress along earlier ones (\(\mathbf d_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H\mathbf d_j = 0\)). Real surfaces are not quadratic, so it is restarted periodically.

L-BFGS. Instead of storing the \(n\times n\) matrix, keep the last \(m\) pairs \((\mathbf s, \mathbf y)\), often 5–20, and apply the update implicitly. Memory and cost per step become order \(mn\), which is what makes optimization of thousands of atoms feasible.

Rational function optimization. Newton's step jumps to any stationary point of the quadratic model (lesson 1.7). RFO solves a slightly enlarged eigenvalue problem whose lowest solution always gives a step that lowers the energy, with a built-in shift that also controls the step length. The partitioned version (P-RFO) maximizes along one mode and minimizes along the rest: the transition-state optimizer of lesson 2.4.

GDIIS. Direct inversion in the iterative subspace, best known from SCF convergence (lesson 3.4), can also extrapolate geometries from previous steps. Some optimizers combine it with quasi-Newton steps near convergence.
