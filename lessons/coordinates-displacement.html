Coordinates and displacement vectors

Goal: describe where an atom is, and describe how it moved, with one small column of numbers each.

Step 1 · See it

An atom on a grid

Imagine an atom in a two-dimensional coordinate system. Its position is

\[ \mathbf r = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

This means the atom is 2 units along the \(x\)-axis and 1 unit along the \(y\)-axis. Now suppose it moves to

\[ \mathbf r' = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \]

Its displacement is the final position minus the starting position:

\[ \Delta\mathbf r = \mathbf r' - \mathbf r = \begin{pmatrix} 3-2 \\ 3-1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \]

The displacement vector tells us both which direction the atom moved and how far. Try moving the atom yourself.

Notice three things while you move it. Each component of \(\Delta\mathbf r\) is just "how far along that axis". A negative component means the atom moved in the negative direction. And the arrow's length depends on both components together.

Step 2 · Calculate it

A worked example by hand

An atom moves from \(\mathbf r = (0.5,\ 1.5)\) to \(\mathbf r' = (2.5,\ 0.0)\). Find its displacement and how far it moved.

  1. Write both positions as columns. \[ \mathbf r = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 1.5 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf r' = \begin{pmatrix} 2.5 \\ 0.0 \end{pmatrix} \]
  2. Subtract component by component: \(x\) from \(x\), \(y\) from \(y\). \[ \Delta\mathbf r = \begin{pmatrix} 2.5 - 0.5 \\ 0.0 - 1.5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2.0 \\ -1.5 \end{pmatrix} \]
  3. Read the signs. \(+2.0\) means it moved 2.0 units in the \(+x\) direction; \(-1.5\) means it moved 1.5 units in the \(-y\) direction.
  4. The two components are the legs of a right triangle, and the displacement is its hypotenuse. By Pythagoras, \[ |\Delta\mathbf r| = \sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2} = \sqrt{2.0^2 + (-1.5)^2} = \sqrt{4.00 + 2.25} = \sqrt{6.25} = 2.5 \]
  5. The atom moved 2.5 units. Squaring removes the sign, so a length is never negative.

In three dimensions nothing new happens. A position has three components and one more term goes under the square root:

\[ |\Delta\mathbf r| = \sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2 + (\Delta z)^2} \]
Position vs displacement. A position depends on where you put the origin. A displacement does not: if you shift the origin, both \(\mathbf r\) and \(\mathbf r'\) shift by the same amount, and the difference stays the same. This is why chemistry programs care about displacements, not raw positions.

Step 3 · Apply it to chemistry

From one atom to a whole molecule

A real atom lives in three dimensions, so it needs three numbers. A molecule with \(N\) atoms needs \(3N\) numbers, and chemistry programs stack them into one long column. For a molecule with 10 atoms, a Cartesian displacement vector has 30 components:

\[ \Delta\mathbf R = \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x_1 \\ \Delta y_1 \\ \Delta z_1 \\ \vdots \\ \Delta x_{10} \\ \Delta y_{10} \\ \Delta z_{10} \end{pmatrix} \]

This single vector describes how every atom moves at once. Two places where you meet it constantly:

  • Geometry optimization. Each optimization cycle proposes one \(\Delta\mathbf R\): a small move of all atoms toward lower energy. The "step size" printed in the output is a length of this vector.
  • Vibrational analysis. A normal mode is a coordinated displacement pattern: a specific \(\Delta\mathbf R\) where all atoms move together in fixed proportions, with mass weighting taken into account.

Read row by row, these nine numbers are \(\Delta\mathbf R\) for water. \(\Delta z = 0\) because the molecule lies in the \(xy\)-plane. Arrows are drawn 4× longer than the real displacement.

In the stretches and the bend, the oxygen moves a little against the hydrogens. That keeps the centre of mass fixed, and it is the first hint of why masses will matter when we reach normal modes. The exact patterns come out of the mass-weighted Hessian, which is where this sequence of lessons is heading.

Where this leads

Why start this small

The objective is not to rush toward eigenvectors. It is to reach a point where, when you see

\[ \mathbf H\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v \]

you know what every symbol represents physically, mathematically and computationally. The \(\mathbf v\) in that equation is a displacement vector of exactly the kind on this page: a column of \(3N\) numbers describing how every atom moves.
